# Landing motion

The landing page has two tracks. The text stays in document flow and the browser scrolls it. The visual stays pinned, and its picture runs toward the scene whose copy the reader is reading: in time, at the page's own pace, and turning without a jump when the reader changes their mind. The page decides only where a scroll ends on desktop; it never decides how far the picture has got from where the page stands.

## Scrolling

The scene being read is named by `onScroll()`. On desktop it is the one the page stands nearest (`restProgress()`), held until the page is `HYST` of a scene past the halfway mark, so a reader resting on a boundary does not turn the picture back and forth. On a phone the copy crossing the picture is the change itself (`progressAt()`): the scene being read becomes the next one as soon as its copy touches the pinned visual, and the previous one as soon as the reader turns back, by the direction the page last moved; a page that stops keeps the scene it asked for. Each scene's rest is `restY()`: on desktop the centre of its copy on the reading line, on a phone the position where the copy before it has just cleared the pinned visual.

Desktop pages the way a paged scroll view does. The reader's own scroll is the browser's. Once their fingers let go (a trackpad's glide, the decaying wheel events a system sends after the fingers lift) or a wheel notch arrives, the page commits to a scene: the one nearest where the glide would end, never short of the next one past where the gesture began, so a short scroll still reaches the next scene and nothing is ever taken back to where it started. The page then carries on at the speed it had and settles there on a critically damped spring (`settleTo()`, `SETTLE_K`), and the rest of that glide is cancelled so it cannot push the page past the scene. The reader's own push is never cancelled: a push the other way, a harder one, a key or a pointer hands the page straight back. A slow drag that ends with no glide is settled once the wheel has been quiet for `QUIET_MS`, onto the next scene if it went `PAGE_MIN` or more, otherwise back where it began. The browser's own snap is not used: under `scroll-snap-type: proximity` a 100 px wheel notch and a short trackpad swipe both went back to where they began, in Chromium and WebKit. `?snap=off` scrolls freely. A wheel over a frame is heard there too (`hearWheel()`), since Safari sends a frame its own wheel events even where that frame ignores the pointer; a frame the reader can use is left to scroll itself.

The keys PageDown, Space and ↓ (and PageUp, Shift+Space and ↑) scroll smoothly to the next (or previous) rest, and Home and End to the top and the close (instantly under reduced motion). The editor demo takes focus to show its caret, so keys arriving in the demos' frames are handled the same until the reader clicks into a demo, after which they are the demo's; typing, a focused control's Space, Shift with an arrow, a control that uses the arrows itself, and scene 5's own scrolling below its rest keep the browser's. The page focuses the editor's frame without scrolling, so the demo's caret never moves the page.

A phone scrolls freely: its copy is longer than its screen. The first screen is the title with scene 1's visual directly below it; scene 1's copy starts one visual band and a reading gap further down, where every later copy starts relative to the visual it reaches. `mobileInkProgress()` measures a scene's copy crossing the pinned visual, 0 when its top touches the visual's bottom and 1 when its bottom clears the visual's top, and `progressAt()` turns those into a position in scenes.

## The chase

The picture is always somewhere along one leg, from scene x to scene y, at `p` between 0 and 1 (`leg`, driven by `chaseFrame()` every frame). Over `[0, PLAY_OUT]` x dissolves into its own wash, over `[PLAY_OUT, PLAY_IN]` the two washes mix, and over `[PLAY_IN, 1]` y gathers out of its wash. `p` runs toward 1 while y is the scene being read and back toward 0 while x is, so a reader who turns back plays the same frames back.

A new target turns the leg from where it stands (`turnTo()`), so the frame on screen never jumps. While x is still dissolving nothing of y is on screen yet, and y is swapped for the new scene. Mid-mix, the mix on screen is baked into one wash (`sim.bake()`), which becomes the outgoing side and mixes on toward the new scene. While y is gathering, y becomes the outgoing side and dissolves again from where it stands. A slow reader therefore sees every scene in turn, and a fast one sees the wash keep turning toward the newest scene and gather only where they stop.

The pace is the page's. The picture is as far through its leg as the page is through its way to the scene it heads for (`leg.track` records where the page stood when the leg last set off toward its present end), so a slow scroll is a slow change and a flick a quick one that lands with the copy. It never moves faster than a leg in `LEG_FAST` seconds, so a dissolve is always seen, nor slower than one in `LEG_SLOW`, so a page that stops short still finishes. On a phone the copy crosses the picture, and while any of it is over the picture the leg holds in the wash (`copyOverPicture()`): it goes no further than `PLAY_IN` toward a scene ahead, nor back past `PLAY_OUT` toward the scene it left, so a scene re-forms only once the copy has left the picture, and copy scrolled back over it dissolves it again. The close is the exception: its film comes up under its own copy. Within a few percent of either end it slows, where the wash's look comes up over the plain print. Measured in Chromium and WebKit: a wheel notch or an ordinary swipe runs about 0.95 s and ends about 0.35 s after the page; a slow drag is followed for its whole length.

A leg waits in its outgoing wash until the incoming scene's recording is whole, and a print nobody has taken is taken there, under the wash (`takeUnderCover()`), with the stage switched to that scene for the capture. A leg starts only from a scene at rest: scene 3's cards, if the reader has moved them, are printed where they were left first (`takeScene3()`). Under reduced motion, with no simulation, or where a print cannot be had, a change cuts, laying the print of the scene being left over the canvas's place and thinning it away while the live scene stands beneath (`dissolveFrom()`).

On desktop the live stage under the canvas changes scene at the leg's midpoint; on a phone the canvas cover rises over the first tenth of a leg and falls over the last, and scene 4's phone zoom follows the leg.

## How a transition looks

Every change is paint on a transparent sheet laid flat on still water: the scene's colour lets go where it lay, opens outward with no direction, thins into the water until little of it is left, and the next scene gathers back out of its own wash the same way, reversed. There is no bare-paper interval and no image that appears without having gathered out of the wash.

The mechanism is the page's shallow-water pigment simulation, recorded once per scene rather than stepped live. `sim.sceneSide()` and `sim.recordSide()` record one scene's print dispersing on a standing film over one shared footprint, water over the whole picture (`footprint()`), so any two scenes' recordings can be played together (`sim.playSides()`), mixed as optical thickness, pigment into pigment, never as pixels. The look is `INK`: no splash and no stirring to one colour; the ink lifts off soon, dense colour runs apart faster than thin (`bloom`, over a second ring of neighbours `reach` cells off), it turns over in small eddies with no direction (`flow`, `flowSize`), and it thins into the water's depth (`dilute`) and fades where it reaches the water's edge. Frames are kept every `recEvery` steps, and recording runs within the per-frame step budget, the incoming side as soon as the leg needs it.

On desktop the title dissolves the same way. It has a small simulation of its own over the heading (`createSim()` builds any instance; the scenes' is `sim`), whose print is every character drawn where the page set it, and it reads the page directly: it is all but gone on the pixel scene 1 rests on, and scrolling back to the top gathers it again from the same frames.

## Scenes 3, 4 and the close

The only solid carryover is the Publish control between scenes 3 and 4. `publishBridge()` copies the real control's rendered styling once, removes its ink from both prints, and moves it continuously between measured composition positions as the leg runs, below the scrolling text and above the pigment. A leg turned toward the other of the two scenes brings the carried control up over the ink it had; a turn away from both lets it fade.

Scene 4 releases every logo in one cascade of about two and a half seconds whose gaps quicken from `ORBIT_GAP_FIRST` to `ORBIT_GAP_LAST`, each a golden angle round the control from the one before, so they bloom evenly around it. Each pops on a damped spring and rocks by `ORBIT_WOBBLE` as it lands. Collision diameter and displayed circle diameter both come from `ORBIT_D`. Arriving in scene 4 starts the logos whenever they are not running, including after a composition was frozen before they ever ran.

The close (scene 5) is a target like any other. Leaving scene 4, its composition freezes where it stands (`deployPrint()`, drawn from the page's own state: each logo's circle, shadow, ring and logo or name, and the Publish control from its own markup) and its logos give up their colour as ink heavier than they show (`CLOSE_DOSE`), slowly (`CLOSE_LIFT`), never thinned, so each bleeds its own colour before the blooms meet. That wash mixes into the film's own print (`closingPigmentPrint()`), which gathers as the film crossfade comes up under the page (`crossAt()`, over the leg's last 55%), fading the visual and whitening scene 4's copy, so the page darkens into scene 5 rather than fading out. The header turns with it: past the middle of the crossfade (`html.nav-dark`, held either side so a page resting there does not flicker) its text turns white and it stands on the close's dark over the next 0.4 s, since the close's own copy scrolls under it; scrolling up out of the close turns it back. A phone's header always has a ground, the page's colour until then; desktop's has none until then. Desktop's visual is pinned in a page that ends before the close does, so while the close holds its composition `holdVisual()` keeps the visual where its pin left it. Back in scene 4, `releaseClose()` lets the composition go and the logos resume.

A page loaded at the bottom stands on the close at once, before the demos have loaded, and while it stands there before boot the film follows the page directly, so a reader who scrolls back up uncovers scene 4 rather than waiting behind the film. The signup link is the one deliberate jump: it is navigation rather than reading, so it stands on scene 5 without a leg (`mossLanding.openSignup`).

On desktop, while scene 2's demo still invites a first publish (the shell's `moss-landing-scene2` class), `publishCue()` sends one hairline ring at a time from its Publish button to the page's far corner, over the art and under the copy, fading as it slows; the next leaves `CUE_REST` after the last has gone. The rings are drawn on one canvas rather than as elements, because an element per ring was a compositor layer of its own and its re-rasterization made the preview's shadow and the Publish pill flicker. Clicking Publish there opens the app's publish receipt for the example site. A resting scene does no work it does not show: the opening's per-frame update writes a root variable or draws the title only when its value changes, and a retake of scene 3's print wakes its sketch and notebook only for its own frames.

## Cost and failure

Each scene's recording is about 27 frames of two half-float textures at the simulation grid's size: about 66 MB on desktop and a quarter of that on a phone, whose grid has a quarter of the pixels. A phone keeps two recordings (a leg's two ends) and desktop three; a baked mix adds one texture. The engine keeps up to ten uploaded prints; one dropped past that is uploaded again the next time a leg plays it. No recording step reads back from the GPU, and neither cached prints nor paused frames are rendered again. Phone captures on first load include only scenes 1 and 2; later media loads as scene 2 approaches. A neighbour's print is taken by switching the live stage under a held canvas, with the cell's transform pinned meanwhile, so a scene-keyed style such as scene 4's phone zoom cannot show over the scene on screen. Canvas renderers expose `canvas.mossCaptureFrame()` to draw and capture in the same turn, avoiding a cleared WebGL drawing buffer.

A transition must never leave the art stuck behind the scroll. A chase frame that throws cuts to the target and sets the `?washdbg` error. A lost WebGL context (a phone short of GPU memory) is detected: the running leg cuts to its target and later changes cut rather than raising a cover over a canvas that no longer draws; when the browser hands the context back, the simulation is built again and a leg in flight lands where it was going. A print that cannot be taken under the wash cuts too. `?washdbg` shows a small readout of the scene shown and asked for, the leg and whether its incoming recording is whole, whether a capture is running, the last capture time and the frame rate. Embedded demo initialization has a deadline and falls back to readable static copy and downloads, also available with JavaScript disabled; until `closing.js` has wired the signup, the page says so instead of offering a form that cannot submit.

Each scene 1 plate has its image's own shape: `PLATE_SET` records every file with its width over height, and the size floor applies to the short side. Scene 1 images and scene 3 artifacts share `currentPrintRect()`: its domain is the union of the actual objects plus pigment padding, including untouched initial positions, and its padding (`INK_ROOM`) is wider than dense ink spreads before it thins, so a wash never meets the canvas's edge and stops there in a straight line. Raster pixel dimensions are capped independently of that domain. Scene 3 retains poses through capture and teardown; the video starts docked above the preview, while external artifacts stay behind it until focused, dragged, or docked. The pigment capture uses that same layer order. The notebook card is composited in paint order (the page's background, its window-chrome image, the rasterized document, then a fresh frame of the live output canvas), so its colours reach the wash. Nested preview photos are decoded before capture and composited with their visible crop and ancestor clipping, which avoids WebKit's SVG foreign-object rasterization dropping decoded images.

## Checking a change

`scripts/check-landing-all.mjs` runs the whole net against one served build. On desktop, `check-landing-desktop-scroll.mjs` drives real wheel input and keys in Chromium and WebKit: notches, nudges, swipes and drags end on a scene past where they began, in their direction, without a long stall or a rebound; a drag too short to count returns; a steady drag is never taken for a glide, and a glide's late tail cannot stop the page short; the reader's own push is never cancelled while the glide is; keys, End, reduced motion, `?snap=off` and the phone width keep their own behaviour. `check-landing-desktop-pace.mjs` holds a leg to the page's pace and its bounds, a leg turned at the dissolve, the mix and the gather without a jump, the film following the close's leg both ways with scene 4 in place, the title's dissolve, scene 2's cue, the scene 3 handoff and scene 4's cascade, and keys that stay with an editor demo the reader has used. On a phone, `check-landing-mobile.mjs` covers native scrolling with no writes to the position, dwell, a leg that sets off as the copy touches the picture and turns back with the reader, a picture that holds in the wash while copy is over it (turned back, stopped mid-crossing, or scrolled back into) and re-forms once the copy has left, a leg that finishes after the reader stops, and reversal; `check-landing-mobile-handoff.mjs` the cover's shape through a leg, the stage and scene 4's zoom under it, no wash at the canvas's bottom or right edge, no window ground over scene 4, a print taken under the cover, and reduced motion cutting; `check-landing-text-track.mjs` the copy staying solid through every leg and the close holding scene 4's composition and letting it go. `check-landing-wash-recovery.mjs` covers a throwing frame, a lost and a restored GPU context, a missing print, a failed capture and a fling past unprinted scenes; `check-landing-header.mjs` the header's text at WCAG AA contrast over what is behind it on the close and back on scene 4, on a phone and on desktop and in every locale; `check-landing-cold-bottom.mjs` a page loaded at the bottom, scrolled back up before boot, and a jump to the bottom; `check-landing-transitions.mjs` every locale's walk through the scenes, the Publish crossing and scene 4's logos, also after scene 4 was left on arrival; `check-landing-readiness.mjs` readiness after a cold jump, and `check-landing-slow-network.mjs` the HTML-only page. Set `PLAYWRIGHT_MODULE` to an existing installation and pass the exact served URL. Inspect the real moss-built output as well as the watcher preview, particularly when testing SVG and iframe capture in WebKit.

These checks establish behaviour, not appearance. A change to how a transition looks is judged on frames: capture the candidate and the current production page at the same scroll positions at a phone and a desktop viewport in WebKit (a continuous scroll as well as fixed steps, forward and back) and compare them side by side before asking anyone else to look. Solver invariants and passing checks do not show that a transition looks right.
