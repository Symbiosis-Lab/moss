# Landing motion

The landing page has two tracks. The text remains in document flow. The visual stays pinned and reads its position from the text; it never advances merely because time passes while a reader pauses. Mobile scrolls freely. On desktop the browser scrolls and snaps the page itself, and only the picture is slowed: the page must rest on the title, on each scene, on the close or on the footer, one gesture cannot pass a rest, and the picture follows the page at a pace slow enough to watch. A browser's snap cannot be slowed, and a script that slows the page has to guess what the fingers are doing, so the page is left alone. The follower position that the picture reads sets off at `WASH_PACE` viewport heights a second the moment the page moves, brakes at `WASH_BRAKE` to arrive still, and is never more than about `WASH_MAX_LAG` seconds behind it; the scene a wash heads for is named from the real page's position between two rests, within a frame, so the wash starts with the gesture. The texts stand `44vh` apart, which keeps a resting scene's neighbours just off the screen, and scene 1's text box is only as tall as its text, so every join is about the same two seconds of wash. With reduced motion the follower is off and the picture reads the page directly.

The page never cancels a wheel or touch event and never writes the scroll position while the reader is scrolling, with one exception: Chrome snaps a short scroll back to the rest it started from (measured on Chrome 154, one 100 px wheel notch moves the page about 90 px within 100 ms and has it back within 200 ms; only two quick notches advance), so a short scroll is carried on to the first rest beyond where the page last stood still, in the gesture's direction. A passive wheel listener adds up a gesture's deltas: one that opens with a notch-sized delta (`NOTCH_PX`, or a line or page delta) is carried at once, and any other once the wheel has been quiet for `ONWARD_QUIET_MS`, if it added up to `ONWARD_MIN_PX`. While a scroll is carried the snap is off, because a scroll to a rest cannot begin until the wheel's own scroll has run, and by then Chrome is already taking the page back (86 px of backward travel, measured); the snap returns once the page has come to rest on a rest. A gesture that has already taken the page nearer the rest after that one, one past the last rest (scene 5's own scrolling) or one above the first is left to the browser. A notch up from the footer is carried onto the close, which Chrome otherwise passes on its way to scene 4. Each scene and the close get a `scroll-margin-top` from `setupSnap()` that puts its snap point exactly on `restY()`; it runs on load, on resize and when fonts land. The keys PageDown, Space and ↓ (and PageUp, Shift+Space and ↑) scroll smoothly to the next (or previous) rest, and Home and End to the top and the close, with no pacing of their own (instantly under reduced motion). The editor demo takes focus to show its caret, so keys arriving in the demos' frames are handled the same until the reader clicks into a demo, after which they are the demo's; typing, a focused control's Space, Shift with an arrow (it extends a selection), a control that uses the arrows itself, and scene 5's own scrolling below its rest keep the browser's. The close rests where the snap puts it, with the footer fully visible there. The page focuses the editor's frame without scrolling, so the demo's caret never moves the page, nor puts back a scroll the reader made.

On a phone the first screen is the title with scene 1's visual directly below it; scene 1's copy starts one visual band and a reading gap further down, where every later copy starts relative to the visual it reaches. Once pinned, `mobileInkProgress()` measures each scene's own text crossing its visual (scene 1 text drives 1→2, scene 2 drives 2→3, scene 3 drives 3→4, scene 4 drives 4→5): dissolution begins when its top touches the visual bottom, and consolidation finishes when its bottom clears the visual top. `progressAt()` turns them into a scene and a fractional transition. On desktop a join's progress is the page between the two rests instead (`washAt()`), so the next scene is whole exactly when its copy has arrived, and `closingProgress()` is either layout's measure for scene 4 → 5.

On desktop the title dissolves too. It has a small simulation of its own over the heading (`createSim()` builds any instance; the scenes' is `sim`), whose print is every character drawn where the page set it. As the reader scrolls to scene 1 its ink blooms and disperses into a wash; late in the recording water floods the whole sheet faster than a pinned rim evaporates, lifts what the film stranded, and flushes it all off (`flood`, `TITLE_RINSE`, `TITLE_LIGHT`), so the paper is clean on the pixel scene 1 rests on. Scrolling back to the top gathers the title again from the same frames.

## How a transition looks

Every watercolor transition follows one sequence: the outgoing scene breaks into pigment, its pigment dissolves into one well-mixed wash, that wash gradually becomes the incoming scene's well-mixed wash, and the incoming scene consolidates into solid by the reverse of its own dispersion. Pigment never leaves the paper in between: there is no bare-paper interval and no image that appears without having gathered out of the wash. Every change comes from water or ink. A wash that is too dark for the copy passing over it is thinned by flushing water through it, not by fading or recolouring the canvas, and the copy stays dark.

The mechanism reuses the page's existing shallow-water pigment simulation rather than stepping it live, on both layouts. `sim.pair()`/`sim.record()` record each print of a boundary dispersing on a standing film (no drying and no uptake) over the sheet the two prints share. After the print breaks up, water is added evenly while the sheet grows outward (the reach per place is precomputed once per boundary into a texture), so the wash spreads and thins. Once mixed, the pigment shader flushes a wash whose mean pigment exceeds `REC_LIGHT`. Frames are kept every `REC_EVERY` steps, and recording runs ahead of the reader within the per-frame step budget, the outgoing side first. `sim.play()` then renders a pure function of position: the outgoing recording forward, the two final washes mixed as optical thickness (pigment into pigment, never pixels), and the incoming recording backward. The same place on the page looks the same whichever way the reader came, and the cover schedule is symmetric in progress. A rest's pixel rounding counts as arrival (the same `.002` as `targetAt`), or a transition resting a hair short of its end would never finish and would hold every later join.

A wash never waits for a print that has not been taken yet. If the incoming print is missing, `pour()` records the outgoing side alone: the outgoing scene dissolves as the copy arrives, and the incoming live scene comes up through the thinning wash as the cover lifts over the second half. The stand-in print is never kept as the incoming scene's. `reachToward()` is the one rule for how far a wash may go, used both to start a join and for each frame's re-point. The Publish bridge still needs both prints. Nothing is captured while the reader crosses a boundary; the warmer captures as soon as the reader stands in a scene.

On desktop, while scene 2's demo still invites a first publish (the shell's `moss-landing-scene2` class), `publishCue()` sends one hairline ring at a time from its Publish button to the page's far corner, over the art and under the copy, fading as it slows; the next leaves `CUE_REST` after the last has gone. The rings are the page's rather than the demo's so they can reach past the preview window, and none leaves while scene 2 is not standing. Clicking Publish there opens the app's publish receipt for the example site: the changes on the ring become its rows, naming the site's real pages, and after a beat the verdict lands the way the app's does, every mark settling to a check and the title fading to "Your site is live". They are drawn on one canvas rather than as elements: an element per ring was a compositor layer of its own, re-rasterized at a new size every frame, and that churn made the preview's shadow and the Publish pill flicker beside it. For the same reason a resting scene does no work it does not show: the opening's per-frame update writes a root variable or draws the title only when its value changes, the demo's Publish ring grows in place, and a retake of scene 3's print wakes its sketch and notebook only for its own frames, then leaves them as the page wants them.

The only solid carryover is the Publish control during scene 3 → 4. `publishBridge()` copies the real control's rendered styling once, removes its ink from the source and target prints, and moves it continuously between measured composition positions. It remains below the scrolling text and above the pigment. On mobile, logos can arrive during this transfer once the preceding text clears the control; they remain solid, without a second wash. Collision diameter and displayed circle diameter both come from `ORBIT_D`. Scene 4 releases every logo in one cascade of about two and a half seconds whose gaps quicken from `ORBIT_GAP_FIRST` to `ORBIT_GAP_LAST`, each a golden angle round the control from the one before, so they bloom evenly around it and a reader who moves on soon still carries all their colour into the close. Each pops on a damped spring and rocks by `ORBIT_WOBBLE` as it lands, and the close's print draws that same rock.

Scene 4 → 5 captures the current logo positions from already-loaded images and plays a recorded pair like every other boundary. The captured print is the live composition's own pixels, so the cover rises over the first `CLOSE_HOLD` of the close while the print holds still; no live layer fades over ink that is already moving, and no logo parked behind the control shows through it. The logos then give up their colour as ink heavier than they show (`CLOSE_DOSE`), slowly (`CLOSE_LIFT` of the usual rate), so each logo bleeds its own colour before the blooms meet. The dose charges colour only: black and grey marks give up their own ink, so the control's dark disc cannot grey the wash, and a near-neutral mark's faint tint is left uncharged, so near-black does not turn to colour. The wash is lightly mixed and never thinned so the colours last, and it turns into the film's own print, which gathers over the rest of the close in step with the crossfade (`xfAt()`, over the close's last 55%) that brings the film up under the page, fades the visual and whitens scene 4's copy, so the page darkens into scene 5 rather than fading out. The print draws each logo as the page does: the circle's shadow and ring, and its logo or, where it has none, its name. The close freezes the composition it captured: while it holds it, the logos neither move nor arrive and no scene change starts or ends the orbit, so through scene 5 and back its print gathers into the same logos. Once the reader is back in scene 4 (or leaves it backwards) `releaseClose()` drops the print and its recording, so the next close takes a fresh one, and the logos resume, the rest still arriving on the beat. Desktop carries the whole close to scene 5's rest and scrolls natively only inside scene 5. Its visual is pinned in a page that ends before the close does, so while the close holds its composition `holdVisual()` holds the visual fixed where its pin left it, and scene 4 dissolves and gathers again in place.

## Cost and failure

Phone cost is bounded: first-load captures include only scenes 1 and 2; later media loads as scene 2 approaches. Each mobile scene print is cached once. A neighbour's print is taken by switching the live stage under a held canvas, with the cell's transform pinned meanwhile, so a scene-keyed style such as scene 4's phone zoom cannot show over the scene on screen. Canvas renderers expose `canvas.mossCaptureFrame()` to draw and capture in the same turn, avoiding a cleared WebGL drawing buffer. The pigment grid uses one quarter of the desktop pixels. One pair of recordings exists at a time (two sides of about 27 frames at the grid's size, about 35 MB on a phone and 140 MB on desktop), plus the desktop title's one side, no recording step reads back from the GPU, and neither cached prints nor paused frames are periodically rendered again.

A transition must never leave the art stuck behind the scroll. A frame of the wash loop that throws ends its transition at the target. A lost WebGL context (a phone short of GPU memory) is detected; the running wash ends at its target and later joins cut rather than raising a cover over a canvas that no longer draws. `?washdbg` shows a small readout of what a join waits on — shown and target scene, running, primed, capturing, the last capture time and the frame rate — for diagnosing a stop on a real phone. Embedded demo initialization has a deadline and falls back to readable static copy and downloads, also available with JavaScript disabled; until `closing.js` has wired the signup, the page says so instead of offering a form that cannot submit.

Each scene 1 plate has its image's own shape: `PLATE_SET` records every file with its width over height, and the size floor applies to the short side. Scene 1 images and scene 3 artifacts share `currentPrintRect()`: its domain is the union of the actual objects plus pigment padding, including untouched initial positions. Raster pixel dimensions are capped independently of that domain, so widening the drawing area does not increase texture dimensions without limit. Scene 3 retains poses through capture and teardown; the video starts docked above the preview, while external artifacts stay behind it until focused, dragged, or docked. The pigment capture uses that same layer order. The notebook card is composited in paint order — the page's background, its window-chrome image, the rasterized document, then a fresh frame of the live output canvas — so its colours reach the wash. Nested preview photos are decoded before capture and composited with their visible crop and ancestor clipping, which avoids WebKit's SVG foreign-object rasterization dropping decoded images.

## Checking a change

`scripts/check-landing-all.mjs` runs the whole net against one served build. The mobile checks cover native scrolling, dwell and reversal (`check-landing-mobile.mjs`), unchanged sources and gradual handoff (`check-landing-mobile-handoff.mjs`), each scene's copy driving its outgoing morph (`check-landing-text-track.mjs`), recovery from a throwing frame, a lost context and a missing print (`check-landing-wash-recovery.mjs`), readiness after a cold jump (`check-landing-readiness.mjs`), and the HTML-only page (`check-landing-slow-network.mjs`). On desktop, `check-landing-desktop-scroll.mjs` drives real wheel notches and keys in Chromium and WebKit (one scene a step, two quick notches one scene, End and the footer, rests that hold with the snap back afterwards, no cancelled wheel event, reduced motion landing at once with the snap kept and the picture not trailing, and in WebKit the wash timings), and `check-landing-desktop-pace.mjs` holds the title's dissolve, the picture's pace, a wash that follows the picture and is whole only on arrival, a close that plays its recording in place all the way to scene 5 and back, and keys that stay with an editor demo the reader has used. Set `PLAYWRIGHT_MODULE` to an existing installation and pass the exact served URL. Inspect the real moss-built output as well as the watcher preview, particularly when testing SVG and iframe capture in WebKit.

These checks establish behaviour, not appearance. A change to how a transition looks is judged on frames: capture the candidate and the current production page at the same scroll positions at a phone and a desktop viewport in WebKit (a continuous scroll as well as fixed steps, forward and back) and compare them side by side before asking anyone else to look. Solver invariants and passing checks do not show that a transition looks right.
