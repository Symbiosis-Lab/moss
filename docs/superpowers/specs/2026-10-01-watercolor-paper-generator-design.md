# Watercolor paper generator

**Date:** 2026-10-01
**Package:** `packages/moss-watercolor`, module `src/paper/`
**Status:** design, awaiting review

## Why

The watercolor engine reads its paper from a 256² texture of seeded value noise: four channels the shaders treat as relief, absorbency, fibre and pore, with no physical origin and no fixed size in the world. Everything a real wash does on real paper — where water runs, how fast the sheet drinks, how an edge frays, where pigment settles — depends on the paper's structure, so a wash simulated over noise can only be tuned to look right, never shown to be right.

This module replaces that texture with a sheet built the way paper is made: fibres deposited from a recipe, pressed against a felt, sized. Every field the solver reads is then derived from that sheet in physical units. The working rule for the whole effort is physics first, looks judged afterwards: the generator is accepted when it reproduces measured paper, and only then does anyone decide whether it is beautiful.

This is the first of five parts. The others, each with its own spec, are: the solver in physical units with resolution as a parameter; physics upgrades that consume these fields (saturation-dependent and anisotropic wicking, pore-gated wet fronts, evaporation-driven edge flow, granulation); a fine-resolution reference simulation for judging the result; and the coarse-grid corrections that let the landing page run the same model in real time. The landing page adopts any of it only after its coarse version beats the live page side by side.

## Non-goals

- No solver changes. The generator feeds today's engine through its existing paper slot, and the engine's default paper stays bit-identical until a host opts in.
- No measurement. The parameters below come from the literature; scans of real sheets (flatbed photometric stereo at 2400 dpi) come later and replace the unverified values.
- No visible paper on a page yet. The lab renders lit relief so the sheet can be inspected; putting texture on a site is a later, separate decision.

## The model

### 1. Furnish

A recipe is a blend of fibre types, each with:

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| length | lognormal, mean and log-spread, mm |
| width | collapsed ribbon width, µm |
| wall thickness | µm |
| fraction | share of the furnish by mass |

Coarseness (mass per length) is derived, not entered: two collapsed walls, `2 · width · wall · ρ_c`, with cellulose wall density `ρ_c = 1.5 g/cm³`. Fibre thickness in the sheet is `2 · wall`. Grammage then fixes how much fibre length is deposited per unit area.

### 2. Deposition

Fibres are deposited one at a time onto a periodic tile, following Niskanen & Alava (1994) as used in the KCL-PAKKA simulator:

- Position and in-plane angle are random. Angle comes from a von Mises distribution whose concentration sets machine-direction bias (zero for mould-made and handmade sheets).
- **Flocculation** (after Provatas et al.): a fibre landing on existing fibres is always accepted; one landing on bare substrate is accepted with probability `p < 1`. Lower `p` gives a cloudier sheet. In the original rule "bare" means no fibre at all, which stops mattering once the first layer is down, and a 300 g/m² sheet has about twenty-five. Here a fibre counts as landing on bare ground when the mass in a box one fibre wide at its centre is less than half the running mean, so clumping keeps acting through the whole sheet. The probe has to be that local: asking the question of the whole fibre footprint averages the ground under a fibre hundreds of cells long, which returns the sheet's own mean, and measured that way the rule fired on 2% of fibres and left formation flat at every setting. One fibre width is the smallest scale at which material-is-here means anything, and unlike a probe fixed in cells it keeps its meaning when the cell size changes. That generalisation is ours, not the paper's.
- **Draping**: the fibre lies as low as possible without penetrating the sheet beneath it, with its height changing by at most `T_f · t_f` over a horizontal distance of one fibre width (`t_f` its thickness). Along the fibre's centreline the resting height is the smallest Lipschitz envelope above the surface under its footprint, computed with one forward and one backward pass. The fibre then raises the surface by its thickness across its footprint and adds its mass. `T_f` is the flexibility parameter, `T_f = (C · w_f · WFF)^(1/4)` in the original; we tune it per furnish.
- Fibres crossing a tile edge wrap around, so the tile repeats without a seam.

Per cell the deposition accumulates mass, top-surface height, and a mass-weighted 2×2 fibre-orientation tensor.

### 3. Felt and press

The felt is generated by the same deposition code from coarse felt fibres (19–43 µm diameter, from the 3–15 denier batt fibres in papermaking felt patents) and low-passed at a conformity length, since a wet sheet follows the felt only at scales larger than its own fibres. The sheet's top surface takes the felt's imprint at a set depth. Pressing then compacts the sheet by a factor and smooths its top surface below a polishing length:

| Finish | Imprint depth | Compaction | Polishing |
|---|---|---|---|
| rough | full | low | none |
| cold-press | partial | medium | short |
| hot-press | small | high | long |

All three are parameters. The felt texture's scale is not entered directly: it follows from the felt fibres and the conformity length, and the presets fit the conformity length so that the imprint's spectral-centroid wavelength lands near the estimated 1 mm. No citable measurement of the felt texture's wavelength or depth exists; these are estimates until scans replace them.

### 4. Sizing

A contact angle per cell, uniform for now. Internal versus surface sizing, and sizing that closes the top pores, are left for later.

### 5. Derived fields

All in SI units, per cell, depth-integrated (the solver is two-dimensional):

| Field | Definition |
|---|---|
| grammage `m` | deposited mass per area |
| thickness `T` | top-surface height after pressing (the sheet's underside is flat) |
| surface height `h` | top surface, also the source of normals for lit relief |
| porosity `ε` | `1 − m / (ρ_c · T)` |
| fibre radius `a` | mass-weighted half-width of the fibres in the cell |
| orientation `A` | the orientation tensor, smoothed over one mean fibre length |
| permeability `K` | `K∥ · A + K⊥ · (I − A)`, with Gebart's (1992) relations at solid fraction `1 − ε`: `K∥ = (8a²/c)(1 − V)³ / V²`, `K⊥ = C · a² · (√(V_max/V) − 1)^(5/2)`, using the hexagonal-packing constants |
| capillary radius `r_c` | `2 · r_h`, with hydraulic radius `r_h = ε · a / (2(1 − ε))` |
| entry pressure `P_e` | `2γ cos θ / r_c` |
| Washburn coefficient `W` | `√(r_c · γ · cos θ / (2μ))`, in m/√s |

Water at 20 °C: `γ = 72.6 mN/m`, `μ = 1.002 mPa·s`.

At 300 g/m² a cotton sheet is about twenty-five fibres deep (its fibres cover the area about twenty-five times over), so single fibres barely shape its surface. Its visible tooth comes from the felt and the formation, while the fibres act through porosity, permeability and how an edge frays. A 32 g/m² xuan sheet is about four fibres deep, and its fibres show directly. The same model gives the two papers their different characters.

## Resolution and tiling

The generator works at a cell size given in micrometres. Two modes share one code path and one recipe.

**Fine mode** runs the full draping deposition on one lattice: a cell of half the narrowest fibre's width (10 µm for cotton, 5 µm for xuan), so every fibre spans two cells. Draping is a lattice model, and its thickness depends on the cell size relative to the fibre width at first order, because any grid locates a fibre's edges only to within a cell. Measured on a 300 g/m² cotton sheet at flexibility 2, the deposited sheet is 500, 620, 670 and 700 µm thick at a half, quarter, eighth and sixteenth of the fibre width, converging towards about 735 µm. Neither of the two rules that might remove the drift does: letting the cross-section conform across its width, and rasterising the exact fibre rectangle, both still drift at first order. The continuum limit is not the more physical answer either, because there a rigid flat fibre that overlaps a neighbour by a sliver lifts its whole width, which a real wet fibre does not. The literature fixes the lattice instead: Niskanen and Alava make a fibre one cell wide, and KCL-PAKKA uses 10 µm cells with fibres one to three cells wide, calibrating flexibility on that lattice. This generator does the same. Fibres two to four cells wide have staircase edges, so relief gradients are over-weighted on the grid axes (measured at 512²: 1.34–1.38 times the isotropic expectation at 0° and 90° for cotton, 1.67–1.73 for xuan). This is a lattice artefact, not fibre physics, and the fine-resolution reference part should judge it. Flexibility is a property of the fibre on this lattice, fitted against measured density and porosity, and a cell finer than the lattice is refused rather than quietly building a thicker sheet. A 20.48 mm tile is 2048² cells. A 300 g/m² cotton tile of that size needs about 350,000 fibres; the draping pass is linear in total fibre length, so the target is under 30 seconds in Node and under 10 seconds for a 1024² tile in a browser worker. The lab's default tile is 512²; 1024² is one choice away in its tile menu.

**Coarse mode** (any larger cell) deposits fibres as mass and orientation only, since draping has no meaning below a cell. Each cell a fibre's centreline crosses gets the share of the fibre's length inside it, which is exactly what averaging a fine sheet over that cell gives. Thickness and porosity then come from a density closure, measured from lattice runs of the same recipe before pressing and stored with the preset together with the cell size it was measured at:
- local density as a linear function of local grammage (the slope depends on the cell size);
- the spread of the thickness that grammage leaves unexplained, and that residual's correlation between neighbouring cells.

Coarse mode multiplies the closure's thickness by one plus correlated noise of that spread. The noise comes from a three-tap kernel solved for the correlation, because the correlation length is under a cell, where a Gaussian blur does nothing. At the engine's texel, cotton's grammage explains under half the thickness variance (correlation 0.67), so without the residual the coarse relief was a third flatter than the fine sheet.

The felt is relative felt grammage, low-passed at the conformity length and expressed in units of its RMS at that scale, measured once on a fine reference tile. A felt generated coarse therefore keeps the lower RMS that averaging gives, rather than being rescaled to unit RMS on each grid, which made the coarse imprint 1.8 times too deep. Felt and pressing are then applied as in fine mode. After resampling, permeability and the other derived fields are recomputed from the resampled mass, thickness, orientation and radius with the same relations, which is also what coarse mode does. Coarse mode is accepted when a fine-mode run box-averaged to the coarse cell size and a coarse-mode run agree in the mean, variance and power spectrum of each field, within tolerances the tests set.

Coarse mode exists because the engine needs large tiles at coarse cells: a landing-page texel is about 2 CSS px, and a CSS pixel is defined as 1/96 inch (0.2646 mm). So the default physical scale is true size, about 0.53 mm per texel and 135 mm for the engine's 256-texel period. Hosts may override the millimetres per CSS pixel.

## Interfaces

```ts
interface PaperRecipe { furnish: FibreType[]; grammage: number; flocculation: number; flexibility: number;
  machineBias: number; felt: FeltSpec; press: PressSpec; contactAngle: number; densityClosure?: DensityClosure }

type Field = Float32Array;          // row-major, periodic, SI units
interface Tensor { xx: Field; xy: Field; yy: Field }
interface PaperSheet {
  width: number; height: number; cell: number;          // cell in metres
  grammage: Field; thickness: Field; surface: Field; porosity: Field; fibreRadius: Field;
  orientation: Tensor; permeability: Tensor;
  capillaryRadius: Field; entryPressure: Field; washburn: Field; contactAngle: Field;
}

generatePaper(recipe: PaperRecipe, grid: { width: number; height: number; cell: number }, seed: number): PaperSheet
resamplePaper(sheet: PaperSheet, cell: number): PaperSheet      // integer factor; conservative box average of mass and thickness, mass-weighted orientation and radius; derived fields recomputed
toEngineChannels(sheet: PaperSheet): { width: number; height: number; data: Uint8Array }  // RGBA for createPaper
shadePaper(sheet: PaperSheet, light: { azimuth: number; elevation: number }, exaggeration = 1): Uint8ClampedArray  // lab only, imported from shade.ts and not exported publicly; elevation 0 is unsupported
```

`toEngineChannels` maps each physical field to the channel whose shader role it plays today, over a fixed physical span exported as `ENGINE_SPANS`: byte 0 is the span's low end and byte 255 its high end, so a shader or the lab can decode a texel, and two papers keep their physical difference. The first version stretched each sheet to its own 0.5th–99.5th percentiles, which gave every preset the same statistics: rough cotton's relief and hot-press's, five times smaller, came out alike. Within one sheet at the engine's texel, absorbency varies by about ±10%, so washes over a physical paper look more even than over today's full-contrast noise. That is the physical answer, and the look is judged afterwards.

| Channel | Shader role today | Physical field |
|---|---|---|
| r relief | flow down the relief, settling into low ground, granulation | thickness about the sheet's mean, ±100 µm |
| g absorbency | uptake rate, lifting, clearing along the grain | Washburn coefficient, 0–32 mm/√s |
| b fibre | lateral wicking of saturation, mist, wet-edge fraying | log₁₀ permeability, 1e-12 to 1e-9 m² |
| a pore | unused today | 128 everywhere: entry pressure shares wicking's capillary radius, so it was absorbency inverted; it returns with pore throats in a later part |

The engine's own `createPaper()` keeps returning today's noise by default. A host opts in by passing the adapter's output.

| `index.ts` | the public exports |

The lab is `packages/moss-watercolor/lab/paper.html` with a small script, served by the repo's existing Vite. Generation runs in a worker. Views: lit relief, grammage, porosity, permeability, orientation (colour by angle, brightness by anisotropy), entry pressure and Washburn coefficient. A radially averaged power spectrum of thickness is always shown; a histogram appears only in the scalar views (not lit relief or orientation). A panel compares the run against the literature rows below. Controls cover the recipe's scalar parameters (grammage, flexibility, flocculation, machine bias, felt imprint depth and conformity, pressing, contact angle) plus cell, tile size and seed, with the presets as starting points; the furnish, the felt fibre and the closures are not exposed. Starting a new run terminates the previous worker, so only the latest result draws.

## Validation

**Unit tests (vitest), each written to fail when its mechanism is removed.** Every test is run once with the mechanism disabled and must go red, or it is deleted.

- Same seed and recipe give byte-identical fields.
- Deposited grammage matches the recipe within 0.5%.
- Periodicity: gradient statistics across the wrap seam match the interior (two-sample test).
- Draping: a stiff fibre (`T_f → 0`) bridges a gap, a flexible one follows it; sheet density rises with `T_f`.
- Flocculation: grammage CV at 1 mm rises monotonically as `p` falls.
- Orientation: an unbiased recipe stays isotropic within tolerance; a biased one recovers the set anisotropy.
- Press: RMS surface roughness orders rough > cold-press > hot-press; a longer conformity length moves the felt's spectral-centroid wavelength longer.
- Fields: Gebart limits hold (`K∥ > K⊥` for aligned fibres; `K` falls as solid fraction rises); porosity stays in (0, 1); the adapter's channels span 0–255 with no NaN.
- Coarse versus fine agreement, as defined above.
- The engine's default paper is unchanged: `createPaper()` without options equals today's bytes.

**Literature checks.** Reported in the lab and in a test that prints, rather than gates, since the targets themselves are uncertain:

- Only porosity is a fitted literature target (the presets' `PRESET_TARGETS`); every other comparison row is a reference value to read against, not something tuned to.
- `xuan-unsized`: porosity 0.755 is fitted. The pore-diameter mode is then a prediction, checked against the measured 14.29 µm. This is the strongest single test of the model, because nothing was tuned to it.
- `xuan-sized`: porosity 0.664 is fitted (through pressing and sizing); 85.2° contact angle is entered.
- `cotton-*`: density 0.5–0.6 g/cm³; in-plane permeability within 10⁻¹³–10⁻¹¹ m² (a derived estimate); Washburn coefficient within 1–10 mm/√s (generic paper data).

**Later, from scans:** height spectrum, RMS roughness and felt wavelength per finish; fibre length and orientation statistics; formation CV. These replace the estimates in the parameter table.

## Sequencing

1. Branch from the engine extraction commit once it lands, so the paper slot (`createPaper`) and the physics preset exist. Until then work stays inside `src/paper/`, which shares no file with the extraction.
2. Build `recipe`, `random`, `deposit`, `fields` under tests first. Then `press`, `resample` and coarse mode, then `engine.ts` and the lab.
3. Fit the presets against the literature targets and record each fitted value with the target it was fitted to.
4. Merge into the package's integration branch. No host changes its paper in this part.

## Risks

- **Felt texture is a guess** until scans exist, and it carries most of what a viewer sees on cotton paper. Mitigation: it is isolated in `press.ts`, parameterised, and labelled as an estimate in every preset.
- **The engine's tuning assumed noise statistics.** Swapping in physical fields through the adapter will change how washes behave even though the channels are normalised the same way. That is expected, and it is why no host opts in during this part.
- **Coarse mode's density closure** may not carry spatial correlations that fine mode has. The agreement test catches this, and the fallback is a lattice run, then resampled. Measured at the engine's texel, it now matches the fine sheet in grammage variance and neighbour correlation, and in thickness variance. Thickness neighbour correlation remains short: 0.35 against 0.56 for cotton and 0.28 against 0.37 for xuan. A fibre bridging over a thick spot lifts its neighbours, so in the fine sheet thickness follows a smoothed grammage rather than the local one. A closure on smoothed grammage is the candidate fix and belongs with the coarse-grid corrections.
- **Pore size is not the measured one.** Fitted to xuan's measured porosity, the modal capillary diameter is 25 µm against the 14.29 µm that mercury porosimetry reports. Porosimetry measures the throats a pore is entered through, which are narrower than the hydraulic radius the generator derives. Entry pressure is set by throats and wicking by the hydraulic radius, so the physics-upgrades part should carry both. Cotton cold-press permeability (2.5e-11 m²) also sits slightly above the derived 1e-13 to 1e-11 m² estimate.
- **The pore-size prediction missed, and no shape factor was added.** At the measured porosity the generated unsized xuan has a modal capillary diameter of 25 µm against the 14.29 µm measured, and a mean of 46 µm in the lab at 512². The closed-form relation was left as it is, so the datum stays an independent check of the model rather than a fitted value. A shape factor, or a separate pore-throat radius for entry pressure, belongs to the physics-upgrades part.
- **Generation time** in a browser may exceed the lab's budget at full tile size. Mitigation: smaller default tiles in the lab; full-size bakes in Node.
